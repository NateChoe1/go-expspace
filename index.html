Crasmaru's ko games encoding

Consider the following go problem. Black to play, with the goal of saving the bottom side group:

Black is locally surrounded; there is no way for black to directly connect the large black blob out to the center of the board where he could hope to make eyes. Black's only option is to aim at capturing one of white's groups, marked with squares. If black goes for that directly by playing at A, white will simply save by capturing a black stone. Therefore, black must save the stone first, although if black tries playing directly at B, white can capture in a ladder that runs into a ko. Therefore, black's only winning move is to capture the ko with C. This makes miai of the two ladders, if white fixes the ladder on the left side of the board, then black will run out the ladder on the right side of the board, and vice versa. Once black's stone is safe, black can come back and capture white stones.

We can extend this problem to add more kos and a more complex ladder. An example of such a problem can be found in Crasmaru's page on Sensei's Library (also viewable here). Let F1 be the boolean formula encoded in the left ladder, and F2 be the formula encoded in the right ladder. Black wins if, at the start of his turn, F1 OR F2. Similarly, white wins if, at the start of her turn, (NOT F1) OR (NOT F2).

Initially, it's black to play and both formulas are false. Black must capture some ko to turn both formulas true. Then, white must capture some ko to turn both formulas false. Both players continue capturing kos until at some point it becomes impossible to flip both boolean formulas, at which point the other player will run out the proper ladder and win the game.

The specific case where F1 = F2 — called the ko game by Robson, but which will hereon be referred to as the symmetric ko game — was shown to be EXPTIME-complete by Robson under Japanese ko rules (Australia National University, TR-CS-82-02, "Exponential Time Decision Problems Relating to KO-like Transition rules"). In Robson's setup, black is aiming for a direct win while white is aiming for a draw by triple ko. The necessity of a triple ko obviously means that this encoding doesn't work with superko. The primary contribution of this proof is the idea to use two different formulas to create stricter conditions on both players.

Motivating two formulas

This is a concept formed early in the process of developing this proof. For various reasons it didn't turn out to be very fruitful, but it provides a good pedagogical foundation for why having two different formulas may be valuable, and it provides a gadget that will be useful in a different context later.

The fact that our boolean formulas must be monotonic is a very strong restriction the setup of the ko game. To circumvent this, we may consider a simulated "ko variable", which is actually made up of three individual kos. In this case, if either player has control of (A AND C) OR (B AND C) they win. This restriction leads to the following three move forced sequence:

(;FF[4]GM[1]CA[UTF-8]AP[besogo:0.0.2-alpha]SZ[19]ST[0]AB[dd][dj][dp][ec][ee][ei][ek][eo][eq][fd][fj]AW[ep][fc][fe][fi][fk][fo][fq][gd][gj][gp]LB[bd:A][bj:B][bp:C];W[ej]TR[bj][bp];B[fp]TR[bd][bq];W[ed])

These three kos effectively act as a single variable. Once a player begins this sequence every move is forced until every variable is flipped and the turn is passed. Notably, ko C is always held by the player who doesn't have control over the variable, so a monotonic boolean formula can use ko C to simulate a NOT gate. There are many issues with this particular approach, one of which being that it must be possible to catch deviations from either player. One potential deviation would be to ignore your opponent's threat and play at some other variable. In the following diagram, ABC forms one compond variable and DEF forms another compound variable.

(;FF[4]GM[1]CA[UTF-8]AP[besogo:0.0.2-alpha]SZ[19]ST[0]AB[dd][dj][dp][ec][ee][ei][ek][eo][eq][fd][fj][np][oc][oe][oi][ok][oo][oq][pd][pj][pp]AW[ep][fc][fe][fi][fk][fo][fq][gd][gj][gp][md][mj][mp][nc][ne][ni][nk][no][nq][od][oj]LB[bd:A][bj:B][bp:C][rd:D][rj:E][rp:F];B[nj];W[ej])

Black's move at ko F is initiating a variable flip. White should respond at ko F to continue the flipping sequence, but white instead plays at ko B creating a threat of her own. Suppose that black were to challenge at this point; who would win? Both players have a set of kos which gives them a reasonable right to challenge and win. If we give priority to black, then any deviations by black would go unpunished. Similarly, if we give priority to white, then any deviations by white would go unpunished. We can fix this by having two different formulas, where one gives priority to black and the other gives priority to white. When one player deviates, the other player gets to choose which formula to get evaluated against and win.

The actual gadgets

This gadget is the 6 ko variable. It is a partisan gadget, this variable is controlled by black, who gets to decide when it gets flipped while keeping the initiative. Black wins if he holds ADEF OR ABEF OR ABCF. White wins if she holds DBC OR DEC. If black is currently holding all of the kos on the left column, then the variable is false. If black is currently holding all of the kos on the right column, then the variable is true. There is exactly one 6 move forcing sequence which flips the value of variable, and even in Japanese rules black is not allowed to flip the same variable twice in a row. Finally, there is exactly one moment in this sequence where it's white to play and she has both kos C and D, which allows white to detect when black is trying to flip a variable and make a challenge if he's not allowed to do so.

We also use that 3 ko variable from earlier to act allow players to pass control of the game to the opponent.

G1

G1 is the simplest EXPTIME-complete game. There are two players X and Y, who each have a set of variables Xi..n, Yi..n, as well as a boolean formula FX(X, Y), FY(X, Y). On each move, players assign some value to each of their variables, where their assignment must satisfy their formula. This game was shown by Robson to be EXPSPACE-complete with superko. We show a direct encoding from G1 to these gadgets which preserves the superko rule.

Encoding G1

Each player has 2n+2 6 ko variables: One copy of your own variables, one copy of your opponent's variables, as well as an 2 extra clock variables called CLOCK1..2, used to prevent cheating. There's also a 3 ko latch to allow players to pass the turn to their opponent. A single move in G1 turns into the following procedure:

  1. Flip CLOCK1
  2. Update your copy of your opponent's variables to match your opponent's, going in order from 1 to n.
  3. Flip CLOCK2
  4. Update your own variables to their new values, going from 1 to n.
  5. Activate the 3 ko latch to pass the turn to the opponent

When black begins his turn, CLOCK_B = CLOCK_W. Then, when black ends his turn (and white begins her turn), CLOCK_B ≠ CLOCK_W. Finally, when white ends her turn (and black begins his turn), CLOCK_B = CLOCK_W

We define the compound term BLACK_FORCING as follows:

BLACK_FORCING = BLACK_TRANSITIONING | BLACK_LATCHING | WHITE_SLOW | WHITE_FAIL | WHITE_BAD_PASS | WHITE_BAD_TRANSITIONING
BLACK_TRANSITIONING = BLACK_TRANS_BLACK | BLACK_TRANS_WHITE
BLACK_TRANS_BLACK = [black has ADEF OR ABEF OR ABCF for some black variable]
BLACK_TRANS_WHITE = [black has DBC OR DEC for some white variable]
BLACK_LATCHING = [black has AC or BC on the latch]
WHITE_SLOW = [black has AB on the latch] AND [black has (ABC OR DEF) for every white variable]

WHITE_FAIL = [it's black's turn to set variables, and WHITE_CLOCK_1 ≠ WHITE_CLOCK2
             [it's black's turn to set variables, black has not yet set CLOCK1, and white's copy of black's variables doesn't match black's copy] |
             [it's black's turn to set variables, black has not yet set CLOCK1, and white has failed to satisfy her boolean formula]

# we can arrange white's variables left to right as CLOCK_W1 X CLOCK_W2 Y
# white must flip these variables from left to right. if there's ever any deviation, we can detect that and give
# black the win.
WHITE_BAD_TRANSITIONING =
  [white is transitioning any variable, and hasn't transitioned CLOCK1 yet] |
  [white is transitioning Xi, but there exists j<i, such that WHITE_Xj ≠ BLACK_Xj] |
  [white is transitioning Xi, white has already set CLOCK2] |
  [white is transitioning CLOCK2, but BLACK_X ≠ WHITE_X] |
  [white is transitioning Yi, white hasn't yet updated CLOCK2] |
  [white is transitioning Yi, but there exists j>i, such that WHITE_Yj ≠ BLACK_Xj] |

We can define the correlary WHITE_FORCING to be the same expression, except that black and white's perspectives are reversed. Intuitively, BLACK_FORCING is true if black is making a forcing move, and WHITE_FORCING is false if white is making a forcing move. In the end, F1 = BLACK_FORCING OR WHITE_FORCING, and F2 = WHITE_FORCING AND BLACK_FORCING. For any deviation, the deviating player will eventually be punished. In the proceeding paragraphs, we will describe a single G1 turn by black, and note after black passes his turn to white she will be in a symmetrical position.

Any deviation by white is immediately punished. Each individual move on a gadget activates BLACK_FORCING, so if white plays anywhere other than on that local gadget, black can immediately run out F1 and win. For the 3 ko latch, there is exactly one possible response to each of black's forcing moves, so there is no possible deviation. For the 6 ko latch, assuming that black is choosing to make a transition which would not violate BLACK_BAD_TRANSITIONING (which would be a mistake anyways), there is no local move by white which would falsify WHITE_FORCING, so for any deviation black can run out F1 and win.

Deviations by black are also punished. If black plays any move which isn't either a black 6 ko variable or a 3 ko latch (i.e. black tries to play on one of white's variables during his turn), black activates BLACK_SLOW, which allows white to run out F2 and win. If black tries to set a variable out of order of the clocks, black is always punished. If black ever sets a variable out of order within a segment between two clock set moves, black is also punished. Finally, if black tries to pass his turn earl, black is punished.

First we examine the period between black setting CLOCK1 and CLOCK2, where black is updating BLACK_Y to match WHITE_Y. Note that black will have some prefix where BLACK_Y = WHITE_Y, and some suffix where black has yet to begin updating those variables. At any point, black should always update the first variable of the suffix. If black tries skipping a variable update and updates some other variable in the suffix, black violates BLACK_BAD_TRANSITIONING and white can challenge. If black tries updating some already-set variable in the prefix, black is not immediately punished; however, after that variable flip the suffix of mismatched variables begins at that newly set variable. Black is not allowed to immediately flip that variable back by the ko rule, black also can't update any other variable in the suffix by the previously described logic, so black is forced to update some earlier value in the prefix. This process continues until black is forced to reset CLOCK1. After this reset, every move becomes an illegal transition by the formula, except for CLOCK1, which is illegal by ko. Finally, if black tries to flip the latch, white can punish through BLACK_FAIL. Therefore, black has no winning move.

Next we examine the period after black sets CLOCK2. Black has some prefix of variables which he has updated, and some suffix which he hasn't yet updated where BLACK_X = WHITE_X. If black tries to update a value in the prefix, it's detected and white can punish with BLACK_BAD_TRANSITIONING. If black turns back and tries to update CLOCK2 after changing some value, that's also detected and punished.

If black tries to flip the 3 ko latch at the immediate start of his turn, black is unable to make the final move due to superko. If black tries to flip the latch in the interregnum between setting CLOCK1 and CLOCK2, white can detect that and punish with BLACK_FAIL.
